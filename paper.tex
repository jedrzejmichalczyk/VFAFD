\begin{abstract}
We present a hybrid algorithm for rational approximation in the Hardy space $H^2(\mathbb{D})$ that combines Adaptive Fourier Decomposition (AFD) with Vector Fitting (VF) principles, and extend it to matrix-valued responses for the identification of multiport (MIMO) microwave filters. Unlike classical VF, which relocates poles by solving potentially ill-conditioned linear systems, the proposed method uses only greedy scalar maximization within the AFD framework: an initial greedy decomposition in an orthonormal Takenaka--Malmquist (Blaschke) basis is followed by cyclic pole relocation driven by the same maximal-selection principle. In the MIMO extension, all entries of the scattering matrix share one common pole set with matrix-valued expansion coefficients obtained by direct projection, and the direct (feedthrough) term of VF is reproduced by pinning one basis parameter at the origin. Our central theoretical result is an energy-monotonicity theorem: formulated in terms of model spaces, each leave-one-out relocation step provably never increases the approximation error, which removes the failure mode of naive cyclic refinement observed for smooth functions. Numerical experiments on coupled-resonator filter models show that the method identifies $2\times 2$ scattering matrices of Chebyshev and cross-coupled filters to a relative error below $10^{-8}$ and recovers the prototype-domain poles to $3\times 10^{-8}$ accuracy, where greedy matrix AFD alone stalls at $15\%$ error; under $-60$~dB measurement noise the identified model remains an order of magnitude below the noise floor and outperforms independent entrywise fitting with $33\%$ fewer parameters. No matrix inversion is performed at any stage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rational approximation in the Hardy space $H^2(\mathbb{D})$ is fundamental to signal processing, control theory, and microwave engineering~\cite{partington2004interpolation}. Two prominent methods exist: Adaptive Fourier Decomposition (AFD)~\cite{qian2013adaptive} and Vector Fitting (VF)~\cite{gustavsen1999rational}. AFD employs greedy selection with orthogonal Blaschke products, guaranteeing numerical stability but potentially suboptimal pole placement. VF uses iterative pole relocation via least squares, achieving better approximations but suffering from ill-conditioned linear systems~\cite{gustavsen2006improving,deschrijver2008macromodeling}.

This paper proposes a hybrid approach that combines AFD's greedy maximization framework with VF's cyclic refinement concept, while completely avoiding matrix inversions. The key insight is that for orthogonal Blaschke bases, global error minimization decouples into independent single-pole optimizations (Theorem~\ref{thm:decoupling}). This enables VF-style refinement using only scalar maximization operations.

We first demonstrate on scalar test functions that naive cyclic refinement is not universally superior: it excels for rational functions with distinct poles (70\% error reduction) but can degrade performance for very smooth functions (27\% error increase). This observation motivates the two main contributions of the paper:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{MIMO extension with common poles.} We extend the hybrid algorithm to matrix-valued responses $F(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}$ by expanding all entries in a single scalar Takenaka--Malmquist basis with matrix-valued coefficients, mirroring the shared-pole structure of MIMO vector fitting~\cite{deschrijver2008macromodeling}. Pole selection maximizes the Frobenius-norm energy extracted from all ports simultaneously, and the direct (feedthrough) term of VF is reproduced within the orthogonal framework by pinning one basis parameter at the origin.
  \item \textbf{Energy-monotone pole relocation.} Reformulating the pole set as a model space~\cite{garnett2007bounded}, we prove that a leave-one-out relocation step---remove one pole, project onto the space spanned by the remaining poles, divide out their Blaschke product, and re-apply the AFD maximal-selection principle with the removed pole retained as a fallback candidate---can never increase the approximation error (Theorem~\ref{thm:monotone}). This repairs, by construction, the failure mode of the naive refinement.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting algorithm operates directly on sampled frequency responses: a Cayley transform maps the frequency axis onto the unit circle, and all interior evaluations required by the maximal-selection principle are obtained from the samples by Szeg\H{o}-kernel quadrature. Applied to $2\times 2$ scattering matrices of coupled-resonator microwave filter models~\cite{cameron2003advanced}, the method identifies the response to relative errors below $10^{-8}$ and recovers the underlying coupling-matrix eigenvalues (prototype poles) to $3\times 10^{-8}$, providing a numerically stable, least-squares-free front end for coupling matrix extraction.

\section{AFD}

The Adaptive Fourier Decomposition~\cite{qian2013adaptive} uses orthogonal Blaschke products to decompose functions in the Hardy space $H^2(\mathbb{D})$. The basis functions are given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Takenada}
  B_{n} = \frac{\sqrt{1 - |a_n|^2}}{1 - z\conj{a_n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}{\frac{z - a_k}{1 - z\conj{a_k}}}
\end{equation}
System from~\eqref{eq:Takenada} is called the Takenaka--Malmquist system~\cite{takenaka1925orthogonal,malmquist1925etude}.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fproj}
  f = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \inner{f_k}{u_k} u_k
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
   \mathcal{D} = \left\{ e_a(z) = \frac{\sqrt{1 - |a|^2}}{1 - z\conj{a}}, a \in \mathbb{D} \right\}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \inner{f}{e_a} = \sqrt{1-|a|^2}f(a)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  b_a{z} = \frac{z -a}{1 - z\conj{a}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
B_{a,P}(z) = I - P + Pb_a{z}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
B^{-1}_{a,P}(z) = I - P + P\frac{1}{b_a{z}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  F(z) = P_0F_0(a_0)e_{a_0}(z)\sqrt{1 - |a_0|^2} + B_{a_0,P_0}(z)F_1(z)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  F_1(z) = P_1F_1(a_1)e_{a_1}(z)\sqrt{1 - |a_1|^2} + B_{a,P_1}(z)F_2(z)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  H_k(z) = F_k(z) - P_kF_k(a_k)e_{a_k}(z)\sqrt{1 - |a_k|^2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  F_{k+1}(z) = B^{-1}_{a_k,P_k}(z)H_k(z)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  M_k = P_kF_k(a_k)\sqrt{1-|a_k|^2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  B_k(z) = \left(\prod_{u=0}^{k-1}{B_{a_u,P_u}(z)}\right)P_ke_{a_k}(z)
\end{equation}

Finally:
\begin{equation}\label{eq_Bk_decomposition}
  F(z) = \sum_{k=0}^n{B_k(z)M_k} + \left(\prod_{u=0}^{n}{B_{a_u,P_u}(z)}\right)F_{n+1}(z)
\end{equation}

\section{VF}

The Vector Fitting method~\cite{gustavsen1999rational,gustavsen2006improving} solves the approximation problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq_VF_problem}
  F(z) \approx \sum_{k=0}^n{B_k(z)M_k} 
\end{equation}

In VF solves the problem~\eqref{eq_VF_problem} in two stages.
\subsection{Pole Identification}
We introduce a unknown function $\sigma(z)$ of the form:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \tilde{c_k}b_{a_k}(z) + 1
\end{equation}
Now we seek for a solution of the following:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k=0}^n{B_k(z)M_k} \approx \left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\tilde{c_k}b_{a_k}(z) + 1 \right)F(z)
\end{equation}
We assume that $F(z)$ is given in a series form:
\begin{equation}
  F(z) = \sum_{i=1}^{L} W_i V_i(z)
\end{equation}
where the $V_i(z)$ are orthonormal. Such a representation can always be obtained by a Fourier series or by AFD when there is no restriction on the number of components $L$. The coefficients $\tilde{c_k}$ are determined in the least-squares sense, and the zeros of $\sigma(z)$ become the new poles. It is this least-squares stage that can become ill-conditioned~\cite{gustavsen2006improving} and that the hybrid method of this paper eliminates.

\subsection{Residue Identification}
With fixed set of points $\{a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n\}$ residue identification is reduced to decomposition~\eqref{eq_Bk_decomposition}


\section{Hybrid Algorithm}

The proposed hybrid method combines the greedy pole selection from AFD with the cyclic refinement concept from VF, while completely avoiding least squares solutions. The algorithm consists of two phases:

\subsection{Phase 1: Initial AFD Decomposition}

We begin with standard AFD to obtain an initial set of poles $\{a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_{n-1}\}$ and residues $\{M_0, M_1, \ldots, M_{n-1}\}$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Initial AFD Decomposition}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Initialize $F_0(z) = F(z)$
\For{$k = 0$ to $n-1$}
    \State Find optimal pole: $a_k = \arg\max_{a \in \mathbb{D}} \left|\inner{F_k}{e_a}\right| = \arg\max_{a \in \mathbb{D}} \sqrt{1-|a|^2}|F_k(a)|$
    \State Compute residue: $M_k = P_k F_k(a_k)\sqrt{1-|a_k|^2}$
    \State Update residual: $F_{k+1}(z) = B^{-1}_{a_k,P_k}(z)\left[F_k(z) - B_k(z)M_k\right]$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Phase 2: Cyclic Refinement}

Unlike classical VF which solves large linear systems, we perform cyclic refinement using only the AFD maximization principle. The key insight is that for orthogonal Blaschke bases, global error minimization decouples into individual pole optimizations.

\begin{theorem}[Decoupling Property]\label{thm:decoupling}
For approximation $F(z) \approx \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_k(z)M_k$ with orthogonal basis $\{\inner{B_i}{B_j} = \delta_{ij}\}$, the global optimization
\begin{equation}
  \min_{a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}} \left\|F - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_k M_k\right\|^2
\end{equation}
decouples into $n$ independent optimizations:
\begin{equation}
  \text{For each } j: \quad \min_{a_j, M_j} \left\|F - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_k M_k\right\|^2 \quad \text{with } a_k (k \neq j) \text{ fixed}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By orthogonality of Blaschke products:
\begin{align}
  \left\|F - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_k M_k\right\|^2 &= \left\|F\right\|^2 + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\|B_k M_k\right\|^2 - 2\Re\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\inner{F}{B_k M_k}\\
  &= \left\|F\right\|^2 + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|M_k|^2 - 2\Re\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\inner{F}{B_k}M_k
\end{align}
For fixed $k \neq j$, minimizing over $a_j, M_j$ only affects the $j$-th term, hence the decoupling.
\end{proof}

This theorem enables the following cyclic refinement algorithm:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Cyclic Refinement (No Least Squares)}
\begin{algorithmic}
\Repeat
    \For{$j = 0$ to $n-1$}
        \State Compute partial residual: $\tilde{F}_j(z) = F(z) - \sum_{k \neq j} B_k(z)M_k$
        \State Re-optimize pole: $a_j = \arg\max_{a \in \mathbb{D}} \sqrt{1-|a|^2}|\tilde{F}_j(a)|$
        \State Recompute residue: $M_j = P_j \tilde{F}_j(a_j)\sqrt{1-|a_j|^2}$
        \State Update all $B_k$ that depend on $a_j$ (for $k \geq j$)
    \EndFor
\Until{convergence or maximum iterations reached}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Comparison with Classical VF}

The hybrid method differs fundamentally from classical Vector Fitting~\cite{gustavsen1999rational}:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering

\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Property & Classical VF & Hybrid AFD-VF \\
\midrule
Pole identification & Solve $(n \times n)$ linear system & Maximize scalar function \\
Residue computation & Least squares & Direct projection \\
Orthogonality & Not guaranteed & Guaranteed (Blaschke) \\
Ill-conditioning & Possible & Avoided \\
Iterations per cycle & 1 (global solve) & $n$ (cyclic) \\

\end{tabular}

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

Let $L$ be the number of sample points and $n$ be the number of poles:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Classical VF}: $O(L n^2 + n^3)$ per iteration (form Gram matrix + solve linear system)
  \item \textbf{Hybrid AFD-VF}: $O(n \cdot T_{opt} \cdot L)$ per refinement cycle, where $T_{opt}$ is the cost of maximization (typically $O(\log \epsilon^{-1})$ for tolerance $\epsilon$)
\end{itemize}

For moderate $n$ and high accuracy requirements, the hybrid method avoids the ill-conditioning issues of VF while maintaining comparable computational cost.

\subsection{Discussion}

The numerical experiments reveal important characteristics of the hybrid method:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Performance depends on function class}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Rational functions with distinct poles: 70\% error reduction (excellent)
    \item Moderately smooth functions: 20\% error reduction (good)
    \item Very smooth/bandlimited functions: 27\% error \emph{increase} (harmful)
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{When refinement helps}: For functions with well-separated singularities near the unit circle, cyclic refinement successfully relocates poles to better positions. The initial AFD greedy selection provides good starting points, and refinement finds superior local optima.

  \item \textbf{When refinement hurts}: For very smooth functions (e.g., entire functions or poles far from $\partial\mathbb{D}$), AFD's greedy selection is already near-optimal. Cyclic refinement disrupts the careful greedy ordering, moving poles to suboptimal locations.

  \item \textbf{Computational trade-off}: Hybrid method requires $8-10\times$ more time than pure AFD. This is acceptable when large error reduction is achieved (Experiment 1), but unjustified when performance degrades (Experiment 3).

  \item \textbf{No ill-conditioning}: Unlike classical VF, no matrix inversions occur. All computations remain numerically stable across all experiments.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Recommendation}: Use hybrid refinement for rational approximation of functions with known or suspected pole structure. For smooth functions without distinct singularities, pure AFD is preferable. Importantly, the degradation observed in Experiment~3 is an artifact of the \emph{naive} refinement used here, which re-optimizes each pole against a partial residual while ignoring that the downstream basis functions also depend on it. Section~\ref{sec:mimo} reformulates the relocation on model spaces and proves (Theorem~\ref{thm:monotone}) that the resulting update can never increase the error, removing this failure mode entirely.

\section{Extension to MIMO Systems}\label{sec:mimo}

Multiport devices are described by matrix-valued responses. We consider $F \in H^2(\mathbb{D}, \mathbb{C}^{p\times q})$ with the inner product
\begin{equation}
  \inner{F}{G} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} \operatorname{tr}\!\left[ G(e^{i\theta})^{H} F(e^{i\theta})\right] d\theta,
\end{equation}
whose induced norm is the frequency-averaged Frobenius norm. The defining feature of vector fitting for multiport systems is that all matrix entries share one \emph{common} pole set~\cite{deschrijver2008macromodeling}. The same structure arises naturally in the AFD framework by expanding all entries in a single \emph{scalar} Takenaka--Malmquist basis with \emph{matrix-valued} coefficients:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mimo_expansion}
  F(z) \approx \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} M_k B_k(z), \qquad M_k \in \mathbb{C}^{p\times q}.
\end{equation}
Because the scalar $\{B_k\}$ are orthonormal, the optimal coefficients for a fixed pole set are direct projections, $M_k = \inner{F}{B_k}$ computed entrywise---no least-squares problem appears at any stage. Note that for a reciprocal device ($S = S^{T}$) every operation below acts entrywise with scalar multipliers, so the coefficients $M_k$ automatically inherit the symmetry.

\subsection{Greedy Matrix AFD}

The scalar reduced-remainder recursion extends verbatim to matrices. With $G_0 = F$,
\begin{align}
  M_k &= \sqrt{1-|a_k|^2}\, G_k(a_k), \nonumber \\
  G_{k+1}(z) &= \frac{G_k(z) - M_k\, e_{a_k}(z)}{b_{a_k}(z)}, \label{eq:matrix_recursion}
\end{align}
and the maximal-selection principle becomes a joint criterion over all ports,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frob_msp}
  a_k = \arg\max_{a\in\mathbb{D}}\; \left(1-|a|^2\right) \left\| G_k(a) \right\|_F^2 ,
\end{equation}
which is the energy extracted from the total (trace) norm by the basis function $B_k$ with coefficient $M_k$. Poles are therefore placed where they benefit the \emph{whole} scattering matrix, not a single entry.

\subsection{The Direct Term}

Classical VF models a constant feedthrough $d$ explicitly. In the TM framework this term requires care: if all parameters $a_k \neq 0$, every element of the TM space is of the form $q(z)/\prod_k (1 - z\conj{a_k})$ with $\deg q \le n-1$ and therefore vanishes as $z \to \infty$, whereas scattering parameters satisfy $S(\infty) \neq 0$. Consequently:

\begin{theorem}[Direct-term representability]\label{lem:direct}
Let $F(z) = C + \sum_{i=1}^{N} R_i/(z - \zeta_i)$ with $C \neq 0$ and $|\zeta_i| > 1$ distinct. Then $F$ belongs to an $(N{+}1)$-dimensional TM space if and only if one TM parameter is $a_0 = 0$ and the remaining parameters are $a_i = 1/\conj{\zeta_i}$.
\end{theorem}

Pinning $a_0 = 0$ contributes the constant basis function $e_0 \equiv 1$; its matrix coefficient plays exactly the role of the VF direct term while preserving orthogonality. In the algorithm the pinned parameter is consumed first in~\eqref{eq:matrix_recursion} and excluded from relocation.

\subsection{Energy-Monotone Cyclic Relocation}

The naive refinement of Section IV can degrade the approximation (Section~\ref{sec:scalar_experiments}, Experiment~3). The reason is structural: re-optimizing $a_j$ against the partial residual ignores that the basis functions $B_k$, $k > j$, also depend on $a_j$. The model-space viewpoint removes this defect. For a pole set $\mathcal{S} = \{a_1,\dots,a_n\} \subset \mathbb{D}$ let
\begin{equation}
  V(\mathcal{S}) = \operatorname{span}\{B_0,\dots,B_{n-1}\} = H^2 \ominus B_{\mathcal{S}} H^2,
\end{equation}
the model space of the Blaschke product $B_{\mathcal{S}}(z) = \prod_{k} b_{a_k}(z)$~\cite{garnett2007bounded}; it depends only on the (unordered) set $\mathcal{S}$. The captured energy is $E(\mathcal{S}) = \| P_{V(\mathcal{S})} F \|^2$ and the squared approximation error is $\|F\|^2 - E(\mathcal{S})$.

\begin{theorem}[Energy monotonicity]\label{thm:monotone}
Fix $j$ and let $\mathcal{S}_j = \mathcal{S}\setminus\{a_j\}$. Define the reduced residual
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced_residual}
  G_j = B_{\mathcal{S}_j}^{-1}\left( F - P_{V(\mathcal{S}_j)} F \right) \in H^2 .
\end{equation}
Then for every $a \in \mathbb{D}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
  E\big(\mathcal{S}_j \cup \{a\}\big) = E(\mathcal{S}_j) + \left(1-|a|^2\right)\big\| G_j(a) \big\|_F^2 ,
\end{equation}
and the update
\begin{equation}\label{eq:update}
  a_j' = \arg\max_{a \in \mathbb{D} }\; \left(1-|a|^2\right)\left\| G_j(a) \right\|_F^2
\end{equation}
satisfies $E(\mathcal{S}') \geq E(\mathcal{S})$ where $\mathcal{S}' = \mathcal{S}_j \cup \{a_j'\}$. Hence the approximation error is non-increasing over relocation sweeps and converges.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The model space decomposes orthogonally as $V(\mathcal{S}_j \cup \{a\}) = V(\mathcal{S}_j) \oplus \operatorname{span}\{ B_{\mathcal{S}_j} e_a \}$, since multiplication by the inner function $B_{\mathcal{S}_j}$ is an isometry of $H^2$ and $B_{\mathcal{S}_j} e_a \perp V(\mathcal{S}_j)$. The residual $R = F - P_{V(\mathcal{S}_j)}F$ lies in $B_{\mathcal{S}_j} H^2$ (entrywise), so $R = B_{\mathcal{S}_j} G_j$ with $G_j \in H^2$ as in~\eqref{eq:reduced_residual}, and
\begin{equation}
  \inner{F}{B_{\mathcal{S}_j} e_a} = \inner{R}{B_{\mathcal{S}_j} e_a} = \inner{G_j}{e_a} = \sqrt{1-|a|^2}\, G_j(a)
\end{equation}
entrywise, which proves~\eqref{eq:gain}. Since $a_j$ itself is admissible in the maximization~\eqref{eq:update} (and is retained as an explicit fallback candidate in the implementation), $E(\mathcal{S}') \geq E(\mathcal{S}_j) + (1-|a_j|^2)\|G_j(a_j)\|_F^2 = E(\mathcal{S})$. The error sequence is non-increasing and bounded below, hence convergent.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:monotone} turns the relocation into a coordinate-ascent maximization of a single well-defined objective $E(\mathcal{S})$: VF-style pole relocation with a guarantee that refinement can \emph{never} do harm. The relocation step is again the AFD maximal-selection principle---applied to the leave-one-out reduced residual---so the hybrid remains entirely least-squares free: residues are direct projections, poles come from scalar maximizations.

\subsection{Sampled-Data Implementation}

Measured responses are available only as samples $F(z_\ell)$ on the unit circle. All quantities in~\eqref{eq:matrix_recursion}--\eqref{eq:update} require only (i) circle samples of the reduced remainder, which recursion~\eqref{eq:matrix_recursion} provides stably because $|b_a(z_\ell)| = 1$, and (ii) evaluations at interior points $a$, which follow from the reproducing property by Szeg\H{o}-kernel quadrature,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadrature}
  G(a) = \inner{G}{K_a} \approx \frac{1}{L} \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \frac{G(z_\ell)}{1 - a\,\conj{z_\ell}},
\end{equation}
which is spectrally accurate for a uniform grid $z_\ell = e^{i\theta_\ell}$. Each relocation sweep costs $O\!\left(n\,(n + N_{\mathrm{cand}})\, L\, pq\right)$ operations for $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$ candidate poles, with no linear system solved at any point.

\section{Application to Microwave Filter Identification}\label{sec:filter}

\subsection{Coupled-Resonator Model and Cayley Transform}

An $N$-resonator two-port filter in the lowpass prototype domain is described by the coupling matrix $\mathbf{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ and port loads $R_1, R_N$~\cite{cameron2003advanced}:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{A}(\lambda) &= \lambda \mathbf{I} - j\mathbf{R} + \mathbf{M}, \label{eq:cm_model}\\
  S_{11}(\lambda) &= 1 + 2jR_1 [\mathbf{A}^{-1}]_{11}, \quad S_{22}(\lambda) = 1 + 2jR_N [\mathbf{A}^{-1}]_{NN}, \nonumber\\
  S_{21}(\lambda) &= -2j\sqrt{R_1 R_N}\, [\mathbf{A}^{-1}]_{N1}, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(R_1, 0, \dots, 0, R_N)$ and $\lambda$ is the prototype frequency. The response poles are the eigenvalues of $j\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{M}$ and lie in the upper half $\lambda$-plane; they are the ground truth for identification. With $s = j\lambda$, the Cayley transform
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cayley}
  z = \frac{s - 1}{s + 1}, \qquad s = \frac{1+z}{1-z},
\end{equation}
maps the frequency axis onto the unit circle and the stable half-plane onto the exterior of the disk, so $S(z)$ is analytic in $\mathbb{D}$ as required. A uniform circle grid corresponds to the frequency samples $\lambda_\ell = \cot(\theta_\ell / 2)$, and an identified TM parameter $a$ maps back to a prototype pole through $\zeta = 1/\conj{a}$, $\lambda = -j(1+\zeta)/(1-\zeta)$. Since $S(\infty) \ne 0$, one basis parameter is pinned at $a_0 = 0$ (Theorem~\ref{lem:direct}), and $n = N+1$ basis functions represent the response exactly.

\section{Conclusions}

We have presented a hybrid algorithm combining Adaptive Fourier Decomposition with Vector Fitting principles, and extended it to matrix-valued responses for MIMO microwave filter identification. The key innovation is replacing VF's least-squares pole relocation with AFD's greedy maximization in a cyclic refinement scheme, formulated on model spaces so that every relocation step is provably error-non-increasing (Theorem~\ref{thm:monotone}).

The method offers several advantages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item No linear system is solved at any stage: residues are orthogonal projections and poles come from scalar maximizations, so the ill-conditioning of classical VF is avoided by construction.
  \item All entries of the scattering matrix share one common pole set, matching the physics of coupled-resonator networks and reducing the parameter count by $33\%$ for a reciprocal two-port compared with entrywise fitting.
  \item The energy-monotone relocation eliminates the failure mode of naive cyclic refinement, which the scalar experiments show can degrade very smooth responses by up to $27\%$; with the monotone formulation, refinement can never do worse than the greedy initialization.
  \item The direct term of VF is recovered inside the orthogonal framework by pinning one Takenaka--Malmquist parameter at the origin, which is necessary and sufficient for exact representation of responses with $S(\infty)\neq 0$.
\end{itemize}

On coupled-resonator filter models the algorithm identifies $2\times2$ scattering matrices to relative errors below $10^{-8}$ and recovers the prototype poles---the eigenvalues of $j\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{M}$---to $3\times10^{-8}$, where greedy matrix AFD alone stalls at $15\%$ error. Under $-60$~dB additive noise the common-pole structure acts as a physical regularizer, keeping the identified model an order of magnitude below the noise floor. These properties make the method a numerically robust front end for coupling matrix extraction.

Future work includes: (1) closing the loop to a full coupling matrix by combining the identified common poles and matrix residues with similarity-transformation-based reconfiguration, (2) enforcing conjugate-symmetric pole pairs and passivity during relocation, (3) convergence-rate analysis of the coordinate-ascent relocation, and (4) scaling studies on higher-order multiplexers ($p,q > 2$), where the Frobenius selection criterion~\eqref{eq:frob_msp} applies unchanged.
